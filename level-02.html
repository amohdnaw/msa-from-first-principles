Repeatability and reproducibility

The same operator twice, or two operators once. One word covers both, they are rarely the same size, and the fix for one does almost nothing for the other. The operator half is also the half a standard study cannot pin.

2.1

Two questions, one word

Level 1 finished with a gauge that has a size, measured by reading one part over and over. what carried overSame bores, same bench micrometer. The part spread is unchanged, so the two levels are describing one process.That is one question, and it is not the only one. Hand the same part to somebody else.

Repeatability is one operator disagreeing with themselves. Reproducibility is operators disagreeing with each other. The plant calls the pair one word, and the word hides the fact that they are rarely the same size and never have the same fix.

2.2

The offset has to persist

A difference between two operators on one part is not evidence of anything: it could be repeatability wearing a costume. What makes reproducibility a separate term is that the offset travels with the person across every part they touch.

Three lines, roughly parallel. the splitrepeatability1.0 µmreproducibility1.8 µmthe gauge term2.06 µmrepeat share of variance24 %Operator A reads low on all ten parts, which is a property of A rather than of any part. That is the whole justification for giving it its own term - and the moment those lines stop being parallel there is a third thing to account for, which is Level 3.

2.3

The same law, and the fix that follows from it

The two terms combine the way everything in this subject combines. Level 1's gauge term was never atomic.

On this gauge reproducibility carries 76 % of the variance, and the two candidate projects are not comparable:

Which is why “fix the gauge” is not advice. and it reversesNothing here is a fact about reproducibility. Make repeatability the larger term and the arithmetic recommends the opposite project.Recalibrating the instrument attacks repeatability; training, a written method and a fixture attack reproducibility. Aim the wrong one and the study will be repeated in six months with the same answer.

2.4

The operator spread is not the operator effect

Now try to measure the split, and the second half turns out to be much harder than the first. Repeatability is easy: every part-operator cell contributes readings, so it arrives with 60 degrees of freedom and about 9 % of error.

Reproducibility looks equally easy - take the spread of the operator averages - and it is a trap. the shape of the errorIt enters divided by the number of readings behind each average, so a bigger study borrows less. It never borrows nothing.Each average is itself made of noisy readings, so its spread carries repeatability inside it. The quantity that estimator actually targets is not the one you wanted:

σop means2=σreprod2+σrepeat2parts×trials\sigma^2_{\text{op means}} = \sigma_{reprod}^2 + \frac{\sigma_{repeat}^2}{parts \times trials}σop means2​=σreprod2​+parts×trialsσrepeat2​​
(2.2)

On a gauge whose repeatability dominates - 3.0 µm against 0.4 - that borrowed term is most of the answer. a number about the wrong thingthe truth0.4 µmwhat the naive estimator targets0.678 µmof that, borrowed repeatability65 %The naive estimator targets 0.678 µm when the truth is 0.4: it overstates by 70 %, and 65 % of what it reports is the instrument rather than the people.

One study cannot show you this. why one study is not evidenceone study said0.260 µm4000 studies, uncorrected0.604 µmagainst the truth+51 %With 3 operators the estimate has 2 degrees of freedom and about 50 % of error, so the seeded study here reported 0.260 - below the truth, in the opposite direction to the bias. Averaged over four thousand studies the bias appears exactly as the algebra says.

2.5

Wrong in both directions at once

The correction subtracts one estimate from another, and the result is not obliged to be a variance. It can come out negative, which is not a coding error: it is an estimator meeting a boundary.

On that noisy gauge it happens 47 % of the time. what zero means hereNot “the operators agree”. “This study cannot tell.” Those are different sentences and only one of them is true.The convention is to report zero - no operator effect - which reads as a finding and is really a shrug.

And clamping is not neutral. the two errorsuncorrected, over 4000 studies+51 %clamped-20 %clamped at zero47 % of studiesTruncating one side of a distribution moves its mean, so the corrected number runs low while the uncorrected one runs high. The same study is wrong in both directions, and picking whichever looks reasonable is not a method.

The verdict tile is the second one: it turns when reproducibility is the term worth attacking. Drag repeatability up and the better fix swaps over. Then pull the study size down and watch the operator spread inflate away from the truth — at four readings it is mostly reporting the instrument, and the correction starts clamping at zero.

So the gauge is split, and the operator half is the half a standard study struggles to see. the seam aheadAn interaction: operators disagreeing about particular parts. It needs ANOVA, and it is often the reason a gauge fails.Worse, everything in this level assumed the operator offsets are parallel - that A reads low by the same amount on every part. Suppose A reads low on the small parts and high on the large ones. Nothing here has a term for that, and average-and-range never will.
