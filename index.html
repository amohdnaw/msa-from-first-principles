A gauge is a process too.

Everything a control chart tells you arrives through a measurement system, and that system has variation of its own. This curriculum takes it apart in seven levels — repeatability against reproducibility, Gage R&R by ANOVA rather than by table lookup, and what a percentage of a percentage is actually claiming. Every constant on every page is computed at render time. Nothing is quoted.

Why a second site

The SPC curriculum derives control limits, capability and detection from first principles across twelve levels. It stops at one boundary on purpose: it never teaches gauge acceptance. This site is the other side of that boundary, and it never teaches control limits.

The method is identical. A claim is made, the arithmetic that supports it is computed in a Python library, the figures and the narration read that library, and a test suite checks the same functions. If a number appears on screen, you can find the function that produced it.

The seven levels

Seven, not twelve. The count comes from the subject rather than from matching the sibling site: measurement systems need seven movements and padding them to twelve would show.

01
Measurement as a process

One bore has one size, and two hundred readings of it have a shape. The spread you observe is never the spread of the parts — and at a real gauge on a real study, that effect is both certain and invisible.

02
Repeatability and reproducibility

The same operator twice, or two operators once. One word covers both, they are rarely the same size, and halving one is worth 3.7 times halving the other. The operator half is also the half a standard study cannot pin: too high uncorrected, too low once clamped.

03
Gage R&R by ANOVA

Level 2 assumed each operator's offset is the same on every part. Break that and there is a fourth term the older arithmetic has no room for. It does not misplace the interaction — it omits it, so the gauge comes out 43 % too small at the far end, looking better than it is.

04
%GRR, ndc, and against what

A verdict is not a variance. Two denominators answer two different questions, so one gauge earns opposite verdicts in two factories — and ndc cannot disagree with either, because it is one of them rearranged. Its gate is 2.9 points tighter than the one printed beside it.

Bias, linearity, stability

Precision is not accuracy. A gauge can repeat beautifully and be wrong, be right in the middle and wrong at the ends, or be right today.
